THE SCIENCE BEHIND THE PRACTICAL

Search time, handling time, evidence.

A humane way to investigate predation and experimental design.

What are we measuring?

A functional response describes how consumption by one predator changes with prey density over a defined period. Here, your visual search and clicks supply the searching behaviour; a fixed delay represents handling.

Holling’s classic experiments included artificial predator–prey tasks. This activity follows that teaching principle: the response emerges from your actions, rather than from an imposed curve.

Holling type II · constant prey densityNe = a N T / (1 + a h N)

Ne = expected number consumed; N = prey per arena; T = trial seconds; a = search coefficient (arena/s); h = handling seconds per prey. The theoretical high-density limit is T/h when h > 0.

Type I is approximately linear before saturation; type II rises with a diminishing slope; type III is sigmoid. A shape alone is not a statistical diagnosis. This game does not force a particular response type.

Why does prey replacement matter?

In removal trials, prey become less abundant as you feed. Rogers’ random-predator equation accounts for this depletion:

Type II · prey not replacedNe = N0 [1 − exp{−a(T − h Ne)}]

The results panel solves this equation for its theoretical overlay in removal mode. Replacement mode uses Holling’s equation. Keep these designs separate when analysing the data.

Run a computer-lab practical

  1. Predict. Sketch consumption against prey density. What should a longer handling time change?
  2. Practise. Use the unrecorded 10-second trial to get used to searching and pointing.
  3. Collect. The default is five densities, three replicate blocks and 20 seconds per trial: five minutes of active searching and handling.
  4. Compare. Create a second experiment with one changed condition, such as 2 s handling. Use the same observer, device, zoom, target size and other settings.
  5. Analyse. Plot raw trials, means and variability. Export to R, jamovi or a spreadsheet for further analysis.
  6. Evaluate. Discuss depletion, learning, fatigue, visibility and why a click experiment cannot establish which natural enemy controls a crop pest best.

A fair and accessible comparison

Use anonymous group codes. Students can share searching, recording and interpretation roles. Touch and large-target trials are supported, but their results should not be pooled uncritically with mouse trials. For students unable to use a visual pointing task, analyse a partner’s exported observations.

Trial order is randomised within each replicate block. Aphid positions change each time and targets do not overlap. The arena is covered while handling, preventing a head start on the next search. Switching tabs or resizing the arena discards the unfinished attempt.

Species and model boundaries

Choose three UK crop pests: peach–potato aphid (Myzus persicae), black bean aphid (Aphis fabae) and pea aphid (Acyrthosiphon pisum). The natural-enemy scenarios are a fourth-instar seven-spot ladybird (Coccinella septempunctata) or a third-instar green lacewing (Chrysoperla carnea group).

Species choices provide biological context. Both predators begin with the same 1 s teaching handling time. Aphid icons have the same target size; colour and visibility can still change human search performance. Images are schematic and are not identification guides.

Published estimates depend on prey and predator stage, temperature, arena and experimental method. Kayahan (2021), for example, reports third-instar lacewing handling estimates of 23.62 min for A. fabae and 21.89 min for A. pisum. Those are estimates from that study, not universal species constants or settings used here.

No temperature response, hunger, prey escape, digestion, predator movement or chemical cues are simulated. Changing the predator label alone does not change capture mechanics. Use the handling control to test an explicitly stated hypothesis.

Replacement, reduction and reflection

This activity replaces live predation for learning about functional responses. It provides repeated practice without maintaining or killing live insects. It does not reproduce every skill or observation available from a live-animal practical. Discuss what is gained, what is lost, and which learning outcomes require another approach.
